Spectrum Reading Grade 5 · Passage

Australia's Giant Toads

How did cane toads get to Australia, and why do people see them as such a pest?

1 Think about toads that you may have seen in the woods, on a hike, or at a zoo. How large do you think they were? Even the largest toads probably were not as big as the cane toad. This toad, native to South America and the Caribbean, can weigh as much as four pounds!

2 In 1935, sugar cane farmers in Australia were having a problem with two types of beetles that were destroying their crops. About one hundred cane toads were shipped to Australia from Hawaii. People hoped the toads would be a solution to the problem. Unfortunately, things turned out very differently than they had anticipated. The cane toads quickly became a more annoying pest than the beetles had ever been.

3 There are several things that make the cane toad so unusual. First of all, the cane toad has almost no natural predators. If the cane toad feels threatened, it will secrete a poisonous liquid. This liquid can kill children and small animals. It can even blind an adult for several hours.

4 The cane toad is also poisonous in all stages of its life. Fish normally feed on the eggs of frogs and toads, but even the eggs of the cane toad are poisonous. Each pair of cane toads can produce more than 30,000 eggs each season. Because many of the eggs and tadpoles are able to mature, the cane toad population can grow very rapidly.

5 This is exactly what happened in Australia. Without any predators, there were suddenly thousands of cane toads in the Queensland area. People might have been more tolerant if the animals had been successful in getting rid of the sugar cane beetles. It turned out, however, that the cane toads did not have any effect on the beetles, which could easily fly out of harm's way.

6 The Australians are not quite sure how to get rid of cane toads. In fact, they continue to spread across the continent. Cane toads eat many types of Australian wildlife. They can also be dangerous to pets and children. Even so, not everyone in Australia hopes that the plans to rid the country of these animals will be successful. Some people even leave food in their backyards or adopt the giant toads as pets!

Section 2 · Language

Numbers in English

The passage you just read is full of numbers: four pounds, 1935, one hundred cane toads, 30,000 eggs, several hours. English uses numbers in many different ways, and each way is said and written a little differently. Study the cards below, then try the exercise.

Use 1 · Counting

Cardinal numbers

Cardinal numbers answer the question "How many?": one, two, three, twenty, one hundred.

  • About one hundred cane toads were shipped to Australia.
  • There are seven continents.
Use 2 · Order

Ordinal numbers

Ordinal numbers answer the question "Which one in the line?": first, second, third, fourth, fifth… They usually come after the.

  • First of all, the cane toad has almost no predators.
  • Queensland is the second largest state in Australia.
  • Short forms: 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 21st, 22nd, 23rd.
Use 3 · Years

Saying years and dates

Years are usually said in two pairs of digits.

WrittenSaid
1935"nineteen thirty-five"
1900"nineteen hundred"
2005"two thousand five"
2026"twenty twenty-six"
August 28"August twenty-eighth"
Use 4 · Clock time

Telling the time

  • 9:00 → "nine o'clock" (o'clock is used only for exact hours)
  • 9:15 → "nine fifteen" or "a quarter past nine"
  • 9:30 → "nine thirty" or "half past nine"
  • 9:45 → "nine forty-five" or "a quarter to ten"
  • Use at with times: at 9:00, at noon.
Use 5 · Big numbers

Thousands, millions, and commas

English puts a comma every three digits from the right: 30,000 and 1,200. (Careful — Indonesian uses a dot here!)

  • 30,000 → "thirty thousand"
  • 1,200 → "one thousand two hundred" or "twelve hundred"
  • Keep hundred, thousand, million singular after a number: two thousand, never "two thousands".
Use 6 · Measurement

Weights, lengths, and amounts

The unit becomes plural after any number bigger than one.

  • It can weigh as much as four pounds.
  • The reef is more than 1,200 miles long.
  • It can blind an adult for several hours.
Use 7 · Number + noun

The hyphen rule

When a number and a noun work together in front of another noun, join them with a hyphen and keep the noun singular.

  • a four-pound toad (not "four-pounds toad")
  • a ten-year-old student
  • But: The toad weighs four pounds. — plural, because it comes after the verb.
Use 8 · Age

Talking about age

  • She is ten years old. (English uses be, not have.)
  • She is ten. — you may drop "years old".
  • a ten-year-old girl — hyphens, and singular year.
Use 9 · Money

Prices

  • The symbol goes before the number: $5, Rp10.000.
  • $5.50 → "five dollars fifty" or "five fifty"
  • It costs five dollars. — plural after a number.
Use 10 · Parts

Fractions, decimals, and percent

  • 1/2 → "one half" · 1/3 → "one third" · 3/4 → "three quarters"
  • 0.5 → "zero point five". Each digit after the point is said separately: 3.14 → "three point one four".
  • 50% → "fifty percent"
Use 11 · Not exact

Approximate numbers

These words tell the reader the number is not exact:

  • about / around / roughly → about one hundred toads
  • more than / over → more than 30,000 eggs
  • nearly / almost / as much as → as much as four pounds
  • several / a few / dozens of / thousands of → several hours, thousands of cane toads
Use 12 · Digit by digit

Codes and phone numbers

Phone numbers, room numbers, and codes are read one digit at a time.

  • 0812 3456 → "oh eight one two, three four five six"
  • 0 is often said as "oh"; 22 may be said "double two".
Words or digits?

When to spell a number out

  1. In ordinary writing, spell out small numbers (one to ten) and use digits for larger ones: four pounds, but 30,000 eggs.
  2. Never begin a sentence with a digit — write "Thirty thousand eggs were laid," not "30,000 eggs were laid."
  3. Use digits for dates, times, prices, measurements in charts, and page numbers.
  4. Be consistent inside one sentence: "We saw 4 toads and 12 beetles," not "four toads and 12 beetles."

Section 3 · Grammar

Past-time grammar

Past simple

A completed past action

Lena found a damaged book yesterday.

Past continuous

An action in progress

They were painting when the table moved.

Past perfect

An earlier past event

They had finished before the rain started.

Past perfect continuous

Duration before a past point

They had been working for three hours.

Past sequence

Place events on a timeline

Use before, after, when, and by the time to show which event happened first.

Used to / would

Past habits and states

Used to can describe old habits or states. Would normally describes repeated actions, not past states.

Be used to

Already familiar

Mrs. Bell was used to busy events. The form after to is a noun or an -ing form.

Get used to

Become familiar

Marcus got used to sorting books. This describes a change from unfamiliar to familiar.

Choosing the form

Use the time clue and the event order

  1. Find markers such as yesterday, while, before, and for three hours.
  2. Decide whether the event was completed, in progress, or earlier than another past event.
  3. Check whether the sentence describes a repeated action, an old state, existing familiarity, or the process of becoming familiar.
