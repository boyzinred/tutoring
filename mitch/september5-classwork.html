Spectrum Reading Grade 5 · Passage

The First Australians

Who are the Aboriginal people of Australia?

1 The Aboriginal people are the original inhabitants of Australia. Archaeologists believe that they have lived in Australia for about 50,000 to 60,000 years! Today, they make up a little more than two percent of the population of Australia.

2 In the past, Aboriginal people were hunters and gatherers. This means that they survived by hunting, fishing, and gathering plants. They did not settle permanently in one place. Instead, they moved around the continent when they needed to refresh their supply of food. The Aboriginal people had, and still have, a strong respect for and connection to the land and nature. It influences almost every part of their culture, from food and shelter, to art and religion.

3 Things quickly changed for the Aboriginal people when Europeans began arriving in Australia in 1788. They brought diseases that the Aboriginal people had never been exposed to before. The Aboriginal people also had to fight for the land that was theirs. There were many years of difficult times. The Aboriginal people had to learn to live in a society that was very different from their ancestors'. They had to fight to keep their culture alive.

4 The situation started to improve for the Aboriginal people in the 1960s. They were finally given the right to vote. Later, the Australian government also began trying to make up for some of the unfair treatment the people had suffered. They returned some of the land to the Aboriginal people that had been taken from them more than a century before.

5 The contributions of Aboriginal people to Australia can be seen in many areas. For example, they created the boomerang, a curved piece of wood that has been used both as a weapon and for sport. Aboriginal rock paintings can be found in many areas of Australia. Some are believed to be 30,000 years old. The didgeridoo is a well-known Australian wind instrument. It is a straight trumpet made from a hollow piece of wood or bamboo. Some people believe it may be one of the world's oldest wind instruments.

6 The Aboriginal people are an important and valuable part of Australian society. Today, many still live a traditional lifestyle in the bush, or Australian wilderness. Others have become a part of modern-day Australian culture and live in cities around the country. Their influences can be found in many aspects of Australian life.

Section 2 · Grammar

Adverbs

The passage you just read is full of adverbs: quickly, permanently, finally, never, still, almost, today, instead. Adverbs are the words that tell you how, when, where, how often, and how much. Work through the guide below from card 1 to card 14 — each card teaches one small idea — and then try the exercise at the end.

Card 1 · The basic idea

What is an adverb?

An adjective describes a noun (a person or thing). An adverb describes almost anything else — most often a verb (an action).

  • Adjective: a quick change → change is a noun.
  • Adverb: Things changed quickly. → changed is a verb.

An easy first test: if the word answers how? when? where? how often? or how much?, it is an adverb.

Card 2 · How?

Adverbs of manner

These tell you how an action happens. Most of them end in -ly.

  • Things changed quickly for the Aboriginal people. (How did things change? Quickly.)
  • They did not settle permanently.
  • She explained the rule clearly.

Adverbs of manner usually come after the verb, or after the verb and its object: He read the passage carefully.

Card 3 · Building them

Making an adverb from an adjective

Start with the adjective and add -ly. There are four small spelling rules:

  • Normal: quick → quickly, careful → carefully, sudden → suddenly
  • Ends in -y: change y to i — happy → happily, easy → easily, angry → angrily
  • Ends in -le: drop the e — simple → simply, gentle → gently, terrible → terribly
  • Ends in -ic: add -ally — basic → basically, tragic → tragically (exception: public → publicly)
Card 4 · The tricky ones

Irregular adverbs

A few common adverbs do not take -ly. Memorise these:

  • good (adjective) → well (adverb): She speaks English well. ✗ speaks good
  • fast → fast: Kangaroos run fast. ✗ fastly
  • hard → hard: They worked hard. (hardly means something completely different — see Card 8.)
  • late → late: He arrived late. (lately means "recently".)
  • early, straight, and daily also keep their own form.
Card 5 · When?

Adverbs of time

These tell you when something happens: today, yesterday, tomorrow, now, then, later, soon, already, finally, still, before, recently.

  • Today, they make up two percent of the population.
  • They were finally given the right to vote.
  • Later, the government began trying to make up for it.

Adverbs of time usually go at the end of the sentence, or at the very beginning followed by a comma.

Card 6 · Where?

Adverbs of place

These tell you where: here, there, everywhere, nowhere, outside, inside, upstairs, underground, around, away, back, abroad.

  • They moved around the continent.
  • The wombat digs underground.
  • Rock paintings can be found everywhere in Australia.

Adverbs of place normally come after the verb (and after its object, if there is one).

Card 7 · How often?

Adverbs of frequency

These tell you how often. They sit on a scale from 100% to 0%:

  • always → usually → often → sometimes → occasionally → rarely → seldom → never

They have a special position rule: before the main verb, but after the verb "be".

  • They had never been exposed to those diseases. (before the main verb)
  • She always arrives on time.
  • He is always on time. ← after is, not before it.
Card 8 · How much?

Adverbs of degree

These tell you how much or to what level: very, quite, really, extremely, almost, nearly, completely, too, enough, a little, hardly.

  • It influences almost every part of their culture.
  • The passage was very interesting.
  • He hardly noticed it. → careful! Hardly means almost not at all, not "in a hard way".

Enough is the odd one: it goes after the word it describes — fast enough, old enough.

Card 9 · Not just verbs

Adverbs can describe adjectives and other adverbs

An adverb of degree can sit in front of an adjective or another adverb to make it stronger or weaker.

  • Adverb + adjective: The didgeridoo is a well-known instrument. / The land is extremely important.
  • Adverb + adverb: They moved very quickly. / She answered quite carefully.

This is why you cannot say they changed quick — after a verb, the word must be the adverb quickly.

Card 10 · Word order

Manner, place, then time

When several adverbs appear together after a verb, English puts them in this order:

  • How → Where → When (manner, place, time)
  • They lived peacefully (how) in the bush (where) for thousands of years (when).

One more rule worth remembering: never put an adverb between a verb and its object. ✗ She speaks well English. ✓ She speaks English well.

Card 11 · Adjective or adverb?

The most common mistake

Use an adjective after the verbs be, seem, look, feel, sound, taste, smell, become. Use an adverb after an action verb.

  • ✓ The music sounds beautiful. ✗ sounds beautifully
  • ✓ She sings beautifully. ✗ sings beautiful
  • ✓ I feel bad about it. ✓ He plays badly.
Card 12 · Comparing

Comparative and superlative adverbs

Short adverbs (usually the irregular ones) add -er / -est. Long -ly adverbs use more / most.

  • fast → faster → the fastest
  • quickly → more quickly → the most quickly
  • Irregular: well → better → best; badly → worse → worst; little → less → least

Use than to compare: A kangaroo travels faster than a wombat.

Card 13 · Linking ideas

Sentence adverbs

Some adverbs describe the whole sentence instead of one verb. They often start the sentence and are followed by a comma.

  • Instead, they moved around the continent.
  • However, life became very difficult.
  • Fortunately, some of the land was returned.
  • Others: Finally, Then, Also, Therefore, Luckily, Suddenly, Of course.
Card 14 · A warning

Not every -ly word is an adverb

Some words end in -ly but are adjectives: friendly, lonely, lovely, silly, ugly, elderly, likely, daily, weekly.

  • ✓ a friendly guide ✗ She smiled friendly. → say in a friendly way.

And some words change meaning completely when you add -ly: hard / hardly, late / lately, near / nearly. Always check the meaning, not just the ending.
