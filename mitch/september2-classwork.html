Spectrum Reading Grade 5 · Passage

Aussie Animals

How are the animals in Australia different from those you might see where you live?

1 Gemma, Kayla, and Vijay were eating lunch on the small patio behind the school's cafeteria. Kayla and Vijay were asking Gemma questions about Australian animals. They couldn't believe that Gemma didn't think it was anything special to see a kangaroo hopping along the side of a road or grazing in a field.

2 "When you see something all the time," Gemma explained, "you hardly even notice it. Do you know how many times you've seen a squirrel, or a deer, or a raccoon in your life?"

3 "Of course not," said Vijay. "But seeing a kangaroo would be completely different."

4 Gemma laughed. "Not if you lived in Australia," she said.

5 "Is it true that a mother kangaroo carries her baby in her pouch?" asked Kayla.

6 "Sure," replied Gemma, pausing to take a bite of her sandwich. "Aussies call baby kangaroos joeys. They live in their mother's pouch until they grow a bit larger. Then, they can take care of themselves and keep up with the others in their group. Did you know that kangaroos can travel more than 30 miles per hour?"

7 "That's pretty fast!" exclaimed Vijay.

8 "Do any other animals carry their babies in a pouch?" wondered Kayla aloud.

9 "The koala and the wombat do," said Gemma. "They are marsupials like the kangaroo."

10 "I know what a koala bear is," said Vijay, "but what is a wombat?"

11 "Well, a koala isn't really a bear," Gemma explained. "People just call them that because they resemble a teddy bear. Koalas spend most of their lives sleeping. There is a type of tree called a eucalyptus that grows in Australia. It's pretty much the only thing that koalas eat. They don't get much energy from the leaves, though, which is why they spend so much time sleeping."

12 "It sounds like they need a change in their diet," laughed Vijay. "Is the wombat similar to the koala and kangaroo?"

13 "The only real similarity is that it is a marsupial, too. Otherwise, the wombat looks like a beaver or a groundhog. It burrows underground and makes tunnels that are 10 to 15 feet long. Some people get wombats as babies and train them. I had a friend who had a very nice pet wombat named Gillian."

14 "Won't you miss the animals in Australia?" asked Kayla. "No one in Massachusetts has a wombat for a pet."

15 Gemma smiled. "I might miss them a little," she said, "but I've never seen snow. I never saw a real raccoon or a deer before I moved here. I also never saw a cardinal or a chickadee. I think Massachusetts is going to be a very interesting place to live."

16 "I guess it all depends on what you are used to," said Vijay. "But I'd take the animals of Australia over a deer any day of the week!"

Section 2 · Language

Numbers in English

The passage you just read is full of numbers: 30 miles per hour, 10 to 15 feet, a bit larger, most of their lives. English uses numbers in many different ways, and each way is said and written a little differently. Study the cards below, then try the exercise.

Use 1 · Counting

Cardinal numbers

Cardinal numbers answer the question "How many?": one, two, three, twenty, one hundred.

  • Three friends were eating lunch on the patio.
  • There are seven continents.
Use 2 · Order

Ordinal numbers

Ordinal numbers answer the question "Which one in the line?": first, second, third, fourth, fifth… They usually come after the.

  • Kayla asks the first question about the pouch.
  • Gemma explains the wombat in the thirteenth paragraph.
  • Short forms: 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 21st, 22nd, 23rd.
Use 3 · Years

Saying years and dates

Years are usually said in two pairs of digits.

WrittenSaid
1935"nineteen thirty-five"
1900"nineteen hundred"
2005"two thousand five"
2026"twenty twenty-six"
September 2"September second"
Use 4 · Clock time

Telling the time

  • 9:00 → "nine o'clock" (o'clock is used only for exact hours)
  • 9:15 → "nine fifteen" or "a quarter past nine"
  • 9:30 → "nine thirty" or "half past nine"
  • 9:45 → "nine forty-five" or "a quarter to ten"
  • Use at with times: at 9:00, at noon.
Use 5 · Big numbers

Thousands, millions, and commas

English puts a comma every three digits from the right: 30,000 and 1,200. (Careful — Indonesian uses a dot here!)

  • 30,000 → "thirty thousand"
  • 1,200 → "one thousand two hundred" or "twelve hundred"
  • Keep hundred, thousand, million singular after a number: two thousand, never "two thousands".
Use 6 · Measurement

Speeds, lengths, and amounts

The unit becomes plural after any number bigger than one.

  • Kangaroos can travel more than 30 miles per hour.
  • A wombat digs tunnels 10 to 15 feet long.
  • A koala may sleep for eighteen hours a day.
Use 7 · Number + noun

The hyphen rule

When a number and a noun work together in front of another noun, join them with a hyphen and keep the noun singular.

  • a fifteen-foot tunnel (not "fifteen-feet tunnel")
  • a ten-year-old student
  • But: The tunnel is fifteen feet long. — plural, because it comes after the verb.
Use 8 · Age

Talking about age

  • She is ten years old. (English uses be, not have.)
  • She is ten. — you may drop "years old".
  • a ten-year-old girl — hyphens, and singular year.
Use 9 · Money

Prices

  • The symbol goes before the number: $5, Rp10.000.
  • $5.50 → "five dollars fifty" or "five fifty"
  • It costs five dollars. — plural after a number.
Use 10 · Parts

Fractions, decimals, and percent

  • 1/2 → "one half" · 1/3 → "one third" · 3/4 → "three quarters"
  • 0.5 → "zero point five". Each digit after the point is said separately: 3.14 → "three point one four".
  • 50% → "fifty percent"
Use 11 · Not exact

Approximate numbers

These words tell the reader the number is not exact:

  • about / around / roughly → about thirty miles per hour
  • more than / over → more than 30 miles per hour
  • nearly / almost / as much as → almost fifteen feet long
  • several / a few / dozens of / thousands of → several hours, thousands of kangaroos
Use 12 · Digit by digit

Codes and phone numbers

Phone numbers, room numbers, and codes are read one digit at a time.

  • 0812 3456 → "oh eight one two, three four five six"
  • 0 is often said as "oh"; 22 may be said "double two".
Words or digits?

When to spell a number out

  1. In ordinary writing, spell out small numbers (one to ten) and use digits for larger ones: three friends, but 30 miles per hour.
  2. Never begin a sentence with a digit — write "Thirty miles per hour is very fast," not "30 miles per hour is very fast."
  3. Use digits for dates, times, prices, measurements in charts, and page numbers.
  4. Be consistent inside one sentence: "We saw 4 koalas and 12 kangaroos," not "four koalas and 12 kangaroos."
